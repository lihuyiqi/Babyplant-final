Succulent

While resurrection angiosperms are drought-tolerant, succulent plants, another branch of angiosperm, use a different survival strategy known as drought avoidance. Instead of evolving to survive the loss of cellular water, succulents have evolved physiologies centered around high capacitance,[16] which is the ability to store large amounts of water in their tissues to prevent their internal system from ever reaching a dehydrated state. Some examples of important physiologies that allow the survival of succulents in dry environments are elastic water storage cells, ribbed stems, wide-band tracheids and a strategy of hydraulic isolation.[16] To begin, succulent organs contain large volumes of specialized storage cells. These cells have a low modulus of elasticity, meaning that their cell walls are highly flexible. This not only allows a large volume for water storage but also allows the plant to release their stored water to their photosynthetic tissues without a large change in its internal water potential. A high-water potential would cause rapid movement of water out of the cells, resulting in dehydration in the cells.[16] Another trait found in many succulents is the ribbed stems. In genera like Ferocactus or Carnigiea, the stem surface is not smooth but convoluted with ribs. These ribs allow the plant to shrink or swell like an accordion as it gains or loses water volume. This prevents the epidermis from rupturing during massive changes in diameter due to different seasonal conditions for water availability.[16] Finally, succulents also have specialized water conducting cells (wide-band tracheids) in their xylem with spirals or ring-like thickenings on their cell walls. These structures provide support to resist conduit collapse when the surrounding tissues contract during drought but also allow flexibility to accommodate the constant change in volume of water.[16]

Survival strategies in succulents

While succulents have high capacitance, they must also prevent the stored water from being pulled back into the dry soil through hydraulic isolation.[16] Internally, controlled cavitation creates air-filled xylem segments that interrupt the water column and block. Externally, root suberization reduces tissue permeability, limiting water movement from the plant back into the soil.[16]
