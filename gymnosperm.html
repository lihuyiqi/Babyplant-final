Evolution

As the sister clade to angiosperms, gymnosperms are seed plants whose evolution has been studied much less extensively. [5] This is due to their long evolutionary history and many extinctions of various species that create huge gaps among the four remaining morphologically distinct extant groups: conifers (Pinophyta), cycads (Cycadophyta), ginkgos (Ginkgophyta), and gnetophytes (Gnetophyta). [5] Nowadays, only 1000 living species remain.[6][7] The origin of seed plants can be traced back to the Devonian period, which was about 400 million years ago. They once attained proliferation during the Mesozoic period (250 million years ago), where a warmer and wetter environment favored their domination on land before their decline in the late Cretaceous period.[5] The detailed figure of the timeline better illustrates the evolution of gymnosperms.

During the Cretaceous period, extreme climate change resulted in major extinctions of gymnosperms and diversification of angiosperms. The North hemisphere has been assumed to have a higher species turnover rate than the South hemisphere since older lineages were replaced by those better adapted to cooler and drier environmental conditions.[9] From ferns that rely on water as pathway of reproduction, gymnosperms went through changes in their water physiology and reproductive adaptations such as seeds, pollen, and wood to survive in different environments.[9]

Water physiology

All gymnosperms except gnetophytes are made solely of tracheids. They are elongated dead cells whose dual function is water transport and mechanical support.[7]

The absence of vessels in gymnosperms leads to a simpler plant structure. In habitats with low soil moisture availability, gymnosperms can improve their hydraulic efficiency by increasing pit membrane diameter and avoid embolism by increasing their wood density and expanding their tracheid diameter.[11] In addition, one important component of the plant cell wall is cellulose; it controls mechanical properties of the cell and enhances plants’ adaptation ability to the environment. Cellulose is biosynthesized by cellulose synthases (CesA), and research has shown that gymnosperms possess a less complex CesA genes’ regulation network than angiosperms. This mechanism is also affected by the quantity of lignin, another component in the plant cell wall, in gymnosperms.[7] Lignin is a hydrophobic carbohydrate molecule which adds support to vascular tissues in stems and prevents water from leaking out of plant tissues.[4] The presence of these components provides insights into the efficiency of gymnosperm plant structure compared to that of angiosperms, while preserving its structural simplicity.

Adaptive Reproductive Mechanisms

Originally, the first form of land plants was ferns and mosses.[9] These ancestors required the presence of water to reproduce since the male gametophyte releases flagellated sperm, which must swim to reach and fertilize the female gamete. The fertilized zygote will later develop into a diploid (2n) sporophyte and produce haploid (n) spores once matured. Ferns and mosses are mostly homosporous; they only produce one type of spore, and their gametophytes mature independently from spores. During their proliferation, which was the Mississippian and Pennsylvanian periods, the climate was mostly wet. However, the following Permian period was dry, and climate change forced the seedless vascular plants to evolve the mechanism of seeds. Seed plants are heterosporous, producing megaspores (female) and microspores (male). The size of their gametophyte is reduced to a microscopic cluster of cells enclosed in the tissues of the sporophyte. The haploid nourishing tissues surround the diploid embryo in the seed to protect it, to furnish necessary amounts of nutrients,[7] and to maintain dormancy for tens or thousands of years, until the growth conditions are optimal.[9] Moreover, the adaptation of pollen also reduced seed plant’s dependence on water for gamete fertilization and development of the embryo. Pollen grains are male gametophytes which can travel long distance with wind-pollination and have a protective coat that prevents the gametophyte from desiccation and mechanical damage.[9] Most importantly, the major difference between the reproductive mechanism of gymnosperms and angiosperms is the enclosure of ovules and seeds; gymnosperms are characterized with naked or partially naked ovules whereas angiosperms have them enclosed in an ovary.[5] The figure below shows the reproduction cycle of pines, a common species of gymnosperms.

Conifers

Conifers represent two-thirds of living gymnosperms and constitute 39% of world forests.[2] With the evolution of seed and pollen, gymnosperms are adapted to live in cold and dry weather, where water is scarce. The narrow shape of their leaves and a thick cuticle prevent water evaporation and explain the phylum’s predominance at high attitudes. While most gymnosperms are dioecious, conifers are mostly monoecious.[4] Their male and female reproductive structures are unisexual and occupy different locations on a same tree. The female structures are at the top of the tree while male structures develop at the base.[3] The sporophylls are developed in different size of strobili, often known as cones, where male cones are smaller than female’s.[4] The far and distinct placements of their reproductive structures also limit the self-pollination in conifers and ensures the genetic diversity of this dominant phylum of gymnosperms.[3]

Ginkgo

The phylum of ginkgophytes is represented by the single surviving species, Ginkgo biloba, which also has the name of “living fossil”. The Ginkgoales first appeared during the lower Jurassic.[4] However, their inefficient physiological plasticity, which is their ability to alter phenotypes and plant structure to a higher fitness level than the original one in extreme conditions,[7] resulted in their extinction during the Triassic-Jurassic mass extinction period. This period is known for massive ecological changes, where the [O2]: [CO2] in the environment significantly decreased.[8] Moreover, one interesting characteristic of ginkgo is its herbivore-free defense mechanism. Ginkgo foliage produces ginkgolides, a potent antifeeding defense. It stops pests from feeding on the tree rather than killing them. Nowadays, it can adapt to environmental pests and pathogen pressure by changing the effectiveness of their defense behavior.[3]
